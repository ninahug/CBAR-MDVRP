\begin{abstract}
Firms that operate several depots increasingly delegate carbon budgets to
those depots for responsibility accounting, and must do so before the demand
of the coming operating cycle is known. Once demand is realised, some depot
accounts are in deficit and others in surplus, and the firm may respond in two
distinct ways: it may move service, by reassigning customers between depots
and reconstructing routes, or it may move carbon responsibility, by
transferring internal credits through an authorised depot-to-depot network and
settling the residual externally. We formulate the resulting problem, the
carbon-budget allocation and recourse multi-depot vehicle routing problem, as
a two-stage stochastic programme in which the per-depot budget vector is the
only non-anticipative decision, and give its deterministic-equivalent
mixed-integer formulation with load-dependent emissions and single-commodity
payload flow. For fixed route emissions, the carbon-account component is a
capacitated minimum-cost transshipment problem. Its dual is an extended price
polyhedron whose depot prices are the marginal cost of emission at each depot,
bounded by the external purchase and sale prices and separated by at most the
friction of the internal transfer path, and whose capacity multipliers
identify congested transfer channels. This structure yields exact marginal
information for the continuous recourse while leaving the integer routing
problem non-convex, and it implies an institutional ordering in which a
frictionless pooled account is never more costly than a network of
capacity-constrained transfers, which in turn is never more costly than
independent depot accounts. We develop a coordination method that alternates
price-guided scenario routing at a fixed budget with an exact linear
programme that re-optimises the budget at fixed scenario emissions, and we
report a computational study that compares it with exact solutions and with
sample-average and progressive-hedging benchmarks, quantifies the value of
allocating budgets stochastically rather than at expected demand, and
identifies the operating conditions under which the two recourse channels
complement rather than substitute for one another.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Carbon performance has moved from an \emph{ex post} disclosure exercise to an
\emph{ex ante} planning constraint. A distribution firm operating several
depots under a corporate emission target increasingly delegates part of that
target to each depot, so that depot managers are evaluated against a budget
rather than against distance or cost alone. The delegation is an accounting
and control device: headquarters remains the legal counterparty in any
external carbon market, but the internal allocation determines how the cost of
compliance is attributed, and therefore how each depot behaves. Because the
operating cycle must be planned before its demand is observed, the allocation
is necessarily made under uncertainty.

This timing creates the problem studied here. Demand is realised after the
budgets are fixed, so some depots exceed their allocation while others fall
short of theirs, and the firm must reconcile the two. It has two very
different instruments for doing so. It may move \emph{service}: reassign
customers that lie between depots and rebuild the affected routes, which
changes physical emissions. Or it may move \emph{responsibility}: transfer
internal carbon credit from a depot in surplus to a depot in deficit through
an authorised channel, and settle whatever imbalance remains by purchasing or
selling externally. The first instrument is operational and changes what the
vehicles do; the second is financial and changes only who is charged. They are
not interchangeable. Internal transfers are neither free nor unlimited, since
approval, audit, and responsibility-adjustment rules restrict them, and
external settlement is executed at a purchase price strictly above the sale
price. A model that omits either instrument, or that lets credit move
frictionlessly, removes the very trade-off that makes the allocation decision
meaningful: if credits could be reshuffled at no cost after demand is
observed, the first-stage allocation would be an accounting convention with no
operational content.

Existing formulations do not capture this structure. Green vehicle routing and
pollution-routing models establish that emissions depend on payload and
sequence rather than distance alone \citep{bektas2011pollution,demir2012alns},
but they treat carbon as an exogenous per-unit penalty and do not model an
allowance that is allocated internally. Carbon-aware operations models
represent cap-and-trade compliance at the level of the firm
\citep{benjaafar2013carbon}, taking the emission total as the decision
variable and leaving unmodelled both the routing decisions that generate it
and the internal attribution of responsibility across facilities. Multi-depot
routing supplies the spatial flexibility that makes reassignment possible
\citep{cordeau1997vrp,montoyatorres2015literature}, but in its classical form
customers are reassigned to reduce distance, not to relocate an emission
liability. Stochastic vehicle routing provides the recourse machinery for
demand realised after commitment \citep{gendreau1996stochastic,
gendreau2016future}; there, however, the first-stage decision is typically a
route plan or a fleet size, whereas here it is a vector of accounting
entitlements whose second-stage value is determined by a market and a transfer
network rather than by a failure-and-replenishment rule. The combination
we study -- a non-anticipative per-depot carbon budget, two structurally
different recourse channels, and a capacity-constrained internal transfer
network -- has not, to our knowledge, been formulated.

Our starting point is a structural observation. Once a scenario's routes are
fixed, the emissions of every depot are determined, and what remains of the
problem is purely an accounting question: how to cover the deficits from the
surpluses at least cost. That residual problem is a capacitated minimum-cost
transshipment problem on the depot network augmented by an external-market
node. Its dual assigns to each depot a price with a direct managerial
meaning -- the marginal cost of one more unit of emission at that depot --
subject to two restrictions: no price may leave the band set by the external
purchase and sale prices, and two depots' prices may not differ by more than
the friction of the cheapest transfer path between them, unless that path is
congested, in which case the congestion carries an explicit multiplier. These
prices are exact local information about the recourse, and they are what a
routing search needs in order to know whether moving a customer between two
depots is worth its distance penalty. They do not, however, convexify the
integer routing problem, and we are careful throughout to claim only the
former.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Model.} We formulate the carbon-budget allocation and recourse
multi-depot vehicle routing problem as a two-stage stochastic programme in
which the per-depot budget vector is the only non-anticipative decision, and
in which operational and accounting recourse are represented explicitly and
separately. We give its deterministic-equivalent mixed-integer formulation
with load-dependent emissions, single-commodity payload flow, depot
eligibility, and a capacitated frictional transfer network, and show that
route duration and depot emissions are determined by the combinatorial
decisions alone.

\item \textbf{Structure.} We characterise the carbon-recourse value function
as a capacitated minimum-cost transshipment problem, derive its dual as an
extended price polyhedron, and establish the resulting price bounds,
path-friction bounds on cross-depot price differences, and complementarity
conditions distinguishing unused, interior, and saturated transfer channels.
We show that the value function is convex, piecewise linear and componentwise
monotone in the deficit vector, and obtain an institutional ordering under
which pooled accounts, a frictional transfer network, and independent depot
accounts are ranked by cost.

\item \textbf{Method and evidence.} We develop a coordination method that
alternates price-guided routing within each scenario at a fixed budget with an
exact linear programme that re-optimises the budget once scenario emissions
are fixed, the latter step being exact rather than heuristic because the
recourse value is convex in the budget for fixed emissions. We evaluate it
against exact solutions on small instances and against sample-average
\citep{kleywegt2002sample} and progressive-hedging
\citep{rockafellar1991scenarios} benchmarks on larger ones, quantify the value
of allocating budgets stochastically rather than at expected demand, and
identify through a factorial design the conditions under which the two
recourse channels complement or substitute for one another.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature_review}

Three literatures bear on the problem studied here. Section~\ref{subsec:carbon_routing}
reviews how carbon has been brought into routing and, separately, into
operational models of regulatory compliance, and explains why an internally
allocated budget is not the same object as an exogenous carbon price or a
single firm-level cap. Section~\ref{subsec:multidepot} reviews multi-depot
routing, the source of the spatial flexibility that this paper turns into a
compliance instrument. Section~\ref{subsec:stochastic_routing} reviews
routing under demand uncertainty and identifies what changes when the
first-stage decision is an accounting entitlement rather than a physical
commitment. Table~\ref{tab:literature_taxonomy} summarises the positioning.

\subsection{Carbon in routing and in operational compliance models}
\label{subsec:carbon_routing}

Emission-aware routing begins from the observation that fuel burn, and hence
carbon, is not proportional to distance. The pollution-routing problem of
\citet{bektas2011pollution} makes fuel consumption an explicit function of
payload and speed, so that the order in which customers are visited changes
emissions even when route length does not. \citet{demir2012alns} develop an
adaptive large neighbourhood search for the resulting non-linear evaluation,
and the surveys of \citet{demir2014review} and \citet{lin2014survey} document
how rapidly the resulting family of models has grown. A parallel branch
constrains the vehicle technology rather than the driving profile:
\citet{erdogan2012green} route alternative-fuel vehicles subject to driving
range and refuelling stops. We adopt the load-dependent emission model of
\citet{bektas2011pollution}, and the property it captures is what gives our
setting its structure: because payload declines along a route, moving a
customer between depots changes not only distance but the emission charged to
each depot's account.

What all of these models share is that carbon is priced exogenously, through
a per-unit cost or a cap on total emissions, and that the price does not
depend on which part of the organisation produced the emission. A separate
literature in operations management models regulatory compliance itself.
\citet{hua2011managing} introduce carbon trading into inventory decisions and
show how the order quantity responds to the carbon price and the cap;
\citet{chen2013carbon} characterise the carbon-constrained economic order
quantity and identify conditions under which emissions can be reduced at
small cost; \citet{benjaafar2013carbon} embed carbon constraints and trading
into procurement, production, and inventory models and show that operational
adjustment is frequently a cheaper compliance channel than technological
investment; \citet{zakeri2015carbon} compare carbon pricing against a
trading scheme at the supply-chain level and find that the two policies
induce materially different operational responses.

This literature works at the level of the firm. Its decision variable is an
aggregate emission quantity, the physical decisions that generate the
emissions are represented in reduced form, and the attribution of
responsibility to individual facilities does not arise. The distinction
matters here, because a single firm-level allowance and a vector of
per-facility budgets induce different optimisation problems. Under one shared
allowance only total emission is priced, and where it is produced is
immaterial to the objective. Under delegated budgets the location of emission
determines which account falls into deficit, and therefore whether a costly
internal transfer or an external purchase is triggered. It is this
localisation of carbon responsibility, together with the frictional channel
that partially undoes it, that the present model adds.

\subsection{Multi-depot routing and the value of reassignment}
\label{subsec:multidepot}

The multi-depot vehicle routing problem couples an assignment decision to a
routing decision: customers situated between depots may be served from
either, and the choice interacts with route length, fleet utilisation, and
workload balance. \citet{renaud1996tabu} and \citet{cordeau1997vrp} establish
the classical tabu-search treatments and the benchmark instances still in
use; \citet{vidal2012hybrid} give a hybrid genetic algorithm that unifies the
multi-depot and periodic variants and remains a state-of-the-art reference;
\citet{montoyatorres2015literature} survey the field, and
\citet{uchoa2017benchmark} discuss the design of instance sets that avoid the
homogeneity of earlier benchmarks, a concern we return to when constructing
test instances.

In all of this work, reassigning a customer is motivated by distance, load
balance, or fleet cost. In the problem studied here it acquires a second and
economically distinct motive: reassignment moves emission from one carbon
account to another, and is therefore a way of relieving a budget deficit
without purchasing anything externally. The same spatial flexibility that the
classical literature exploits for cost reduction becomes, under delegated
carbon budgets, a compliance instrument. This is why the model must represent
both the physical channel and the accounting channel explicitly rather than
collapsing them: they relieve the same imbalance through different
mechanisms and at different costs, and which one is preferable depends on
the geometry of the instance and on the friction of the transfer network.

\subsection{Routing under demand uncertainty}
\label{subsec:stochastic_routing}

When demand is realised after commitment, routing becomes a stochastic
programme with recourse. \citet{bertsimas1992vehicle} analyses a priori
routing under stochastic demand; \citet{gendreau1996stochastic} survey the
early literature; \citet{laporte2002integer} give an integer L-shaped method
for the capacitated problem with stochastic demands; and
\citet{oyola2018stochastic} and \citet{gendreau2016future} review models and
research directions. The standard formulations commit to a route plan or a
fleet, observe demand, and repair infeasibility, typically through return
trips to a depot or through route reoptimisation. On the methodological
side, solution approaches rely on sample average approximation, which
replaces the expectation by an empirical average over sampled scenarios and
provides statistical guarantees on the resulting estimate
\citep{kleywegt2002sample}, or on scenario decomposition such as progressive
hedging, which solves each scenario separately and drives the copies of the
first-stage decision towards consensus \citep{rockafellar1991scenarios}. We
use both as benchmarks, and follow \citet{birge2011introduction} in reporting
the value of the stochastic solution and the expected value of perfect
information.

Our first-stage decision differs from the one these formulations assume. It
is not a physical commitment such as a route or a vehicle, but a vector of
accounting entitlements, and its second-stage consequences are determined by
a market and a transfer network rather than by a failure-and-replenishment
rule. Two implications follow. First, the recourse contains a component that
is independent of routing: once emissions are fixed, settling the accounts is
a minimum-cost flow problem whose value function and duals can be computed
exactly, which is what permits the budget to be re-optimised exactly rather
than heuristically. Second, whether the first-stage decision is worth
optimising under uncertainty at all becomes an empirical question rather than
an assumption, and we report it against deterministic and simple managerial
allocation rules rather than presuming that the stochastic model dominates.

On the algorithmic side, adaptive large neighbourhood search is the standard
substrate for routing problems at this scale
\citep{ropke2006adaptive,pisinger2007general}, including for emission-aware
variants \citep{demir2012alns}, and we adopt it for the routing subproblem. Its
operator selection is driven by search history and is therefore uninformed
about the state of the carbon accounts. Matheuristics that combine such a
search with an exact mathematical-programming component are by now a
well-established design \citep{archetti2014survey}, the canonical device
being set-partitioning reoptimisation over a pool of generated routes
\citep{subramanian2013hybrid}. The method developed here places its exact
component differently. Rather than reoptimising routes, it solves the
first-stage budget problem exactly at fixed scenario emissions, which is
possible because the recourse value is convex in the budget; and it draws
guidance for the routing search from the dual of the recourse problem, so
that each depot carries a price and candidate moves are screened by the price
difference between the depots they connect. Every shortlisted move is
nevertheless accepted only after re-evaluation under the complete objective,
so the guidance accelerates the search without altering what counts as an
improvement.

\section{Problem Description and Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

\subsection{Problem description and assumptions}
\label{subsec:problem_description}

We consider a distribution firm that operates a set of depots
$\mathcal{D}$ under a centrally coordinated carbon-management programme.
The depots are operational units with separate carbon-performance accounts,
but they do not behave strategically: headquarters remains the single
decision maker and the legal counterparty in the external carbon market.
Before the demand of the next operating cycle is observed, headquarters
delegates a binding internal carbon budget to each depot.  The allocation is
used for responsibility accounting, performance evaluation, and the internal
assignment of external carbon costs; it is not a statutory allowance held by
the depot itself.

Once demand is realised, headquarters jointly determines the customer--depot
assignments and the vehicle routes.  The emissions of every route are charged
to the depot at which the route is rooted.  Demand uncertainty may therefore
leave some depot accounts in deficit and others in surplus relative to their
ex ante budgets.  The firm has two distinct classes of recourse.  First,
\emph{operational recourse} reallocates eligible boundary customers and
reconstructs vehicle routes, thereby changing physical emissions.  Second,
\emph{accounting recourse} transfers internal carbon credits through an
authorised depot-to-depot network and settles any residual account positions
through external purchases or sales.  Internal transfers incur directional
friction costs and are capacity limited, representing approval, accounting,
responsibility-adjustment, and internal-control restrictions.  External
transactions are executed centrally, but their costs or proceeds are assigned
to the depot accounts that generate the residual positions.

This distinction is material.  The ex ante budgets cannot be costlessly reset
after demand is observed; otherwise the first-stage allocation would be a
pure accounting convention.  Likewise, depot surpluses and deficits cannot be
netted outside the authorised internal-transfer network; otherwise the
transfer variables would be redundant.  The firm must consequently decide
where to place carbon responsibility before demand is known and, after demand
is observed, whether an imbalance should be corrected by moving service,
moving internal carbon credits, or trading externally.  We refer to the
resulting problem as the \emph{corporate carbon-budget allocation and recourse
multi-depot vehicle routing problem} (CBAR-MDVRP).

The decision sequence is as follows.  In the first stage, headquarters chooses
a budget vector $B=(B_d)_{d\in\mathcal D}$.  A demand scenario
$\omega\in\mathcal W$ is then realised.  In the second stage, the firm chooses
the customer assignments, vehicle routes, route payloads, depot emissions,
internal transfers, and external trades for that scenario.  Only $B$ is
non-anticipative; all routing and recourse decisions are scenario dependent.

The problem can now be stated compactly.  Given a set of depots with known
locations, fleets and carbon accounts, a set of customers with known locations
and depot eligibility, a finite set of demand scenarios with known
probabilities, an authorised internal transfer network with directional
frictions and capacities, and external purchase and sale prices, the
CBAR-MDVRP is to determine (i) a non-anticipative allocation of the corporate
carbon budget across depots and, for each demand scenario, (ii) an assignment
of customers to eligible depots, (iii) a set of feasible vehicle routes, and
(iv) the internal carbon-credit transfers and external trades that settle the
resulting depot accounts, so as to minimise the expected sum of dispatch,
travel, internal-transfer and external-settlement costs.

\paragraph{Assumptions}
The model rests on the following assumptions, stated explicitly because
several of them delimit what the formulation is intended to represent.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(A\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Centralised decision making.} Headquarters is the single
decision maker and the sole legal counterparty in the external carbon market.
Depots are responsibility centres and do not act strategically, so the
allocation is a control instrument rather than the outcome of a negotiation
or an internal auction.

\item \textbf{Binding first-stage allocation.} Depot budgets are chosen before
demand is observed and are not revised once it is. Were they costlessly
revisable, the first-stage decision would carry no operational content.

\item \textbf{Restricted internal transfer.} Carbon responsibility moves
between depots only along authorised arcs, at a directional friction cost and
subject to a capacity limit, representing approval, audit and internal-control
requirements. A frictionless uncapacitated network is retained only as an
institutional regime.

\item \textbf{Exogenous carbon prices with a positive spread.} External
purchase and sale prices are exogenous, constant across scenarios, and satisfy
$p^{\mathrm{buy}}>p^{\mathrm{sell}}\ge0$. The firm is a price taker, which is
appropriate for a single distribution firm in an established compliance
market.

\item \textbf{Scenario-invariant structure.} Customer locations, depot
eligibility, arc data, fleet parameters and carbon prices are identical across
scenarios; only customer demands vary. Demand uncertainty is represented by a
finite scenario set with known probabilities.

\item \textbf{Complete recourse.} Every scenario admits a feasible
second-stage solution: each customer can be served by at least one eligible
vehicle, and the available fleet can cover realised demand. Together with
external trading at finite prices, this makes the recourse problem feasible
and bounded for every first-stage decision.
\end{enumerate}

Assumption (A6) concerns operational feasibility only. It does not assume that
any depot can meet its carbon budget, which is precisely the imbalance the
model is built to resolve.

\subsection{Mathematical model}
\label{subsec:mathematical_formulation}

\paragraph{Notation}

The distribution system is defined on a directed graph
$\mathcal G=(\mathcal V,\mathcal A)$.  The customer set is $\mathcal C$ and the
depot set is $\mathcal D$.  Depot $d$ is represented by an origin node $o_d$
and a terminal node $r_d$.  The fleet is
$\mathcal K=\bigcup_{d\in\mathcal D}\mathcal K_d$, where $\mathcal K_d$ is the
set of vehicles stationed at depot $d$.  Vehicles may be grouped into classes
of identical units, $\mathcal K_d=\biguplus_{t\in\mathcal T_d}\mathcal K_{d,t}$;
$t(k)$ denotes the class of vehicle $k$.  Vehicles in the same class share
capacity $Q_k$, fixed dispatch cost $F_k$, maximum route duration $H_k$, and
empty- and full-load fuel rates $\rho_k^0$ and $\rho_k^Q$.  Heterogeneity is
retained as a modelling capability rather than as a separate contribution.

Each customer $i\in\mathcal C$ has a nonempty set of eligible depots
$\Omega_i\subseteq\mathcal D$.  Its home depot $h(i)\in\Omega_i$ is fixed,
and a customer with $|\Omega_i|\ge2$ is a boundary customer.  As in the
original formulation, eligibility may be generated by the distance-ratio rule
\begin{equation}
\Omega_i=\{h(i)\}\cup
\left\{d'\in\mathcal D\setminus\{h(i)\}:\;
\frac{d_{i,h(i)}}{d_{i,d'}}\ge\chi_\Omega\right\},
\qquad \chi_\Omega\in(0,1].
\label{eq:depot_flexibility}
\end{equation}
For vehicle $k$, let $d(k)$ denote its home depot,
$o_k=o_{d(k)}$, $r_k=r_{d(k)}$, and
$\mathcal C_k=\{i\in\mathcal C:d(k)\in\Omega_i\}$.  Its node and arc sets are
\begin{align*}
\mathcal V_k&=\mathcal C_k\cup\{o_k,r_k\},\\
\mathcal A_k&=\{(o_k,j):j\in\mathcal C_k\}
\cup\{(i,j):i,j\in\mathcal C_k,\ i\ne j\}
\cup\{(i,r_k):i\in\mathcal C_k\}.
\end{align*}
Every arc $(i,j)$ has distance $d_{ij}>0$, travel time $\tau_{ij}\ge0$, and
baseline operating cost $c_{ij}\ge0$.  Customer $i$ has service duration
$\sigma_i\ge0$.

Demand uncertainty is represented by a finite scenario set $\mathcal W$.
Scenario $\omega$ occurs with probability $\pi_\omega>0$, where
$\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal W}\pi_\omega=1$, and customer $i$ requires
$q_i^\omega>0$ units.  Customer locations, eligibility sets, arc data, fleet
parameters, and carbon-market prices do not vary across scenarios.  We assume
that every scenario is operationally feasible, in particular that each
customer can be served by at least one eligible vehicle and that the available
fleet can cover the realised demand.

The corporate carbon budget is $B^{\mathrm{corp}}$.  Headquarters chooses
$B_d$ for depot $d$, subject to lower and upper responsibility bounds
$\underline B_d$ and $\overline B_d$.  We assume
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}\underline B_d
\le B^{\mathrm{corp}}
\le\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}\overline B_d.
\end{equation*}
One unit of internal carbon credit covers one unit of route emission.

Internal transfers use the directed network
$\mathcal G^T=(\mathcal D,\mathcal A^T)$.  For
$a=(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T$, $\kappa_{dd'}\ge0$ is the unit transfer friction
and $\overline T_{dd'}\ge0$ is the transfer capacity.  Define the outgoing and
incoming transfer-arc sets
\begin{equation*}
\delta_T^+(d)=\{(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T\},
\qquad
\delta_T^-(d)=\{(d',d)\in\mathcal A^T\}.
\end{equation*}
Residual deficits are purchased externally at $p^{\mathrm{buy}}$, and residual
surpluses are sold at $p^{\mathrm{sell}}$, with
\begin{equation}
p^{\mathrm{buy}}>p^{\mathrm{sell}}\ge0.
\label{eq:market_spread}
\end{equation}
The strict spread rules out simultaneous purchase and sale as an optimal
accounting action and prevents external-market arbitrage.

\paragraph{Deterministic-equivalent formulation}

The first-stage variable is $B_d\ge0$.  For every scenario $\omega$, the
binary variables are $y_{id}^\omega$ (customer--depot assignment),
$z_i^{k\omega}$ (vehicle service), $x_{ij}^{k\omega}$ (arc use), and
$a_k^\omega$ (vehicle dispatch).  The nonnegative continuous variables are
$f_{ij}^{k\omega}$ (payload), $E_d^\omega$ (depot emission),
$t_{dd'}^\omega$ (internal transfer), $b_d^\omega$ (external purchase), and
$s_d^\omega$ (external sale).

The deterministic equivalent is
\begin{align}
\min\quad Z={}&
\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal W}\pi_\omega\Bigg[
\sum_{k\in\mathcal K}F_k a_k^\omega
+\sum_{k\in\mathcal K}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal A_k}
 c_{ij}x_{ij}^{k\omega}
\notag\\[-1mm]
&\hspace{29mm}
+\sum_{(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T}\kappa_{dd'}t_{dd'}^\omega
+p^{\mathrm{buy}}\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}b_d^\omega
-p^{\mathrm{sell}}\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}s_d^\omega
\Bigg]
\label{eq:obj}
\end{align}
subject to the first-stage budget constraints
\begin{align}
&\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}B_d=B^{\mathrm{corp}},
\label{eq:budget_total}\\
&\underline B_d\le B_d\le\overline B_d,
&&\forall d\in\mathcal D,
\label{eq:budget_bounds}
\end{align}
and, for every $\omega\in\mathcal W$, the routing constraints
\begin{align}
&\sum_{d\in\Omega_i}y_{id}^\omega=1,
&&\forall i\in\mathcal C,
\label{eq:c1}\\
&\sum_{k\in\mathcal K_d}z_i^{k\omega}=y_{id}^\omega,
&&\forall i\in\mathcal C,\ \forall d\in\Omega_i,
\label{eq:c2}\\
&z_i^{k\omega}\le a_k^\omega,
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,\ \forall i\in\mathcal C_k,
\label{eq:c3}\\
&\sum_{i\in\mathcal C_k}z_i^{k\omega}\ge a_k^\omega,
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,
\label{eq:c4}\\
&\sum_{j\in\mathcal C_k}x_{o_kj}^{k\omega}=a_k^\omega,
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,
\label{eq:c5}\\
&\sum_{i\in\mathcal C_k}x_{ir_k}^{k\omega}=a_k^\omega,
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,
\label{eq:c6}\\
&\sum_{j\in\mathcal C_k\cup\{r_k\}\setminus\{i\}}
 x_{ij}^{k\omega}=z_i^{k\omega},
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,\ \forall i\in\mathcal C_k,
\label{eq:c7}\\
&\sum_{j\in\mathcal C_k\cup\{o_k\}\setminus\{i\}}
 x_{ji}^{k\omega}=z_i^{k\omega},
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,\ \forall i\in\mathcal C_k,
\label{eq:c78}\\
&\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal A_k}\tau_{ij}x_{ij}^{k\omega}
 +\sum_{i\in\mathcal C_k}\sigma_i z_i^{k\omega}
 \le H_k a_k^\omega,
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,
\label{eq:c12}
\end{align}
together with the single-commodity payload-flow constraints
\begin{align}
&\sum_{j\in\mathcal C_k}f_{o_kj}^{k\omega}
 =\sum_{i\in\mathcal C_k}q_i^\omega z_i^{k\omega},
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,
\label{eq:c16}\\
&\sum_{j\in\mathcal C_k\cup\{o_k\}\setminus\{i\}}
 f_{ji}^{k\omega}
 -\sum_{j\in\mathcal C_k\cup\{r_k\}\setminus\{i\}}
 f_{ij}^{k\omega}
 =q_i^\omega z_i^{k\omega},
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,\ \forall i\in\mathcal C_k,
\label{eq:c17}\\
&0\le f_{ij}^{k\omega}\le Q_k x_{ij}^{k\omega},
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,\ \forall(i,j)\in\mathcal A_k,
\label{eq:c19a}\\
&f_{ir_k}^{k\omega}=0,
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,\ \forall i\in\mathcal C_k.
\label{eq:c19b}
\end{align}
Because every $q_i^\omega$ is strictly positive, the payload-flow balance
excludes a customer subtour disconnected from the vehicle origin: summing
\eqref{eq:c17} over such a subtour would give zero net boundary flow but a
strictly positive total demand.

The load-dependent emission charged to depot $d$ is
\begin{align}
E_d^\omega={}&
\sum_{k\in\mathcal K_d}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal A_k}
\theta d_{ij}\left[
\rho_k^0x_{ij}^{k\omega}
+\frac{\rho_k^Q-\rho_k^0}{Q_k}f_{ij}^{k\omega}
\right],
&&\forall d\in\mathcal D,
\label{eq:c20}
\end{align}
where $\theta>0$ converts fuel use into carbon emission and
$\rho_k^Q\ge\rho_k^0\ge0$.  The first term prices empty-vehicle distance,
whereas the second term captures the additional effect of the payload carried
on each arc.  Hence customer sequencing affects emissions even when route
distance is unchanged.

Internal transfers and depot carbon accounts satisfy
\begin{align}
&0\le t_{dd'}^\omega\le\overline T_{dd'},
&&\forall(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T,
\label{eq:transfer_capacity}\\
&B_d
+\sum_{(h,d)\in\delta_T^-(d)}t_{hd}^\omega
+b_d^\omega
=E_d^\omega
+\sum_{(d,h)\in\delta_T^+(d)}t_{dh}^\omega
+s_d^\omega,
&&\forall d\in\mathcal D.
\label{eq:account_balance}
\end{align}
The left-hand side of \eqref{eq:account_balance} is the carbon credit available
to depot $d$: its ex ante budget, incoming internal transfers, and external
purchases.  The right-hand side is its use of credit: route emissions,
outgoing transfers, and external sales.  The account equation is the only
constraint that directly connects the first-stage allocation to the
scenario-wise physical and financial recourse decisions.

The variable domains are
\begin{align}
&B_d\ge0,
&&\forall d\in\mathcal D,
\notag\\
&E_d^\omega,b_d^\omega,s_d^\omega\ge0,
&&\forall d\in\mathcal D,\ \forall\omega\in\mathcal W,
\notag\\
&x_{ij}^{k\omega},y_{id}^\omega,z_i^{k\omega},a_k^\omega\in\{0,1\},
&&\text{for all applicable }i,j,k,d,\omega,
\notag\\
&f_{ij}^{k\omega}\ge0,
\qquad
t_{dd'}^\omega\ge0,
&&\forall\omega\in\mathcal W,\ \forall k,\ (i,j)\in\mathcal A_k,\ (d,d')\in\mathcal A^T.
\label{eq:c_domain}
\end{align}
All second-stage constraints above are imposed for every
$\omega\in\mathcal W$; the scenario quantifier is suppressed after the first
line of each block to improve readability.

The objective \eqref{eq:obj} is measured in monetary units.  Its first two
terms are dispatch and travel costs, the third is the internal organisational
cost of reallocating carbon responsibility, and the last two terms are the
cost and revenue assigned to depot accounts from external trading.  Since
$p^{\mathrm{buy}}>p^{\mathrm{sell}}$, simultaneous external purchase and sale
at one depot cannot be optimal: reducing both variables by their smaller value
preserves \eqref{eq:account_balance} and strictly reduces the objective by the
buy--sell spread.  No additional binary market-status variable is required.

\begin{proposition}[Complexity]
\label{prop:complexity}
The CBAR-MDVRP is $\mathcal{NP}$-hard in the strong sense.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Consider an instance with a single scenario of probability one, a single
depot, a corporate budget large enough that
$\underline B_d\le B^{\mathrm{corp}}$ is satisfied with
$B_d^{\phantom{|}}\ge E_d$ at any feasible routing, and
$p^{\mathrm{sell}}=0$. The carbon account is then slack at every solution,
the transfer and trading variables are zero at optimality, and the objective
reduces to dispatch and travel cost over capacitated routes rooted at one
depot. This is the capacitated vehicle routing problem, which is
$\mathcal{NP}$-hard in the strong sense. Allowing several depots and several
scenarios only enlarges the instance class.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:complexity} concerns the routing layer, which the model
inherits. The carbon layer does not by itself create the difficulty: for
fixed routing, Section~\ref{sec:carbon_recourse} shows that what remains is a
linear programme. The interaction of the two layers is what the solution
method in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} is designed to exploit.

\paragraph{Scenario-wise combinatorial core}
\label{subsec:reduction}

\begin{corollary}[Scenario-wise combinatorial core]
\label{cor:core}
Fix a scenario $\omega$ and a feasible binary routing plan
$(x^\omega,y^\omega,z^\omega,a^\omega)$.  Along each used route, the payload on
an arc is uniquely equal to the sum of the demands of the customers that
remain to be served.  Consequently the route duration and every depot emission
$E_d^\omega$ are uniquely determined by the customer assignments, route
partition, and customer sequences.  For a fixed first-stage budget $B$, the
remaining carbon-account contribution is the optimal value of the linear
recourse problem defined in Section~\ref{sec:carbon_recourse}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Starting from the zero payload on the terminal arc, equations
\eqref{eq:c17}--\eqref{eq:c19b} determine the payloads backwards along each
route as suffix demands; \eqref{eq:c16} is then satisfied at the origin.
Substitution in \eqref{eq:c20} determines depot emissions.  Route duration is
the sum appearing in \eqref{eq:c12}.  Once $B$ and $E^\omega$ are fixed, only
the internal-transfer and external-trading variables remain in the carbon
accounts, and their minimum cost is the recourse value introduced below.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Valid inequalities and symmetry breaking}
\label{subsec:strengthening}

The scenario-wise arc-load inequalities
\begin{align}
&f_{ij}^{k\omega}\ge q_j^\omega x_{ij}^{k\omega},
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,\ \forall(i,j)\in\mathcal A_k:\ j\in\mathcal C_k,
\label{eq:vi1}\\
&f_{ij}^{k\omega}\le(Q_k-q_i^\omega)x_{ij}^{k\omega},
&&\forall k\in\mathcal K,\ \forall(i,j)\in\mathcal A_k:\ i\in\mathcal C_k,
\label{eq:vi2}
\end{align}
strengthen the linear relaxation.  The first requires the payload entering
customer $j$ to include its demand; the second recognises that the demand of
customer $i$ has been delivered before an outgoing arc is traversed.  For
consecutive identical vehicles $k<k'$ in the same depot and class, the
scenario-wise ordering
\begin{equation}
a_k^\omega\ge a_{k'}^\omega,
\qquad
\forall d\in\mathcal D,\ \forall t\in\mathcal T_d,\ \forall\omega\in\mathcal W,
\quad k<k'\text{ consecutive in }\mathcal K_{d,t}.
\label{eq:vi3}
\end{equation}
removes dispatch-index symmetry without excluding all representatives of any
physical solution.  Inequalities \eqref{eq:vi1}--\eqref{eq:vi3} are valid:
they are satisfied by every optimal solution of the formulation above, and
are added to tighten its linear relaxation rather than to alter the set of
feasible routing plans.

\subsection{Compact two-stage form and special cases}
\label{subsec:model_interpretation}

Let $\mathcal X(q^\omega)$ denote the feasible set of the scenario routing and
payload variables defined by \eqref{eq:c1}--\eqref{eq:c20}.  Let
$C_{\mathrm{op}}(S^\omega)$ be the dispatch and travel cost of a routing plan
$S^\omega\in\mathcal X(q^\omega)$, and let
$E(S^\omega)=(E_d(S^\omega))_{d\in\mathcal D}$.  The extensive-form model can
be written compactly as
\begin{equation}
\min_{B\in\mathcal B}
\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal W}\pi_\omega
\min_{S^\omega\in\mathcal X(q^\omega)}
\left\{
C_{\mathrm{op}}(S^\omega)
+\Psi_{\overline T}\bigl(E(S^\omega)-B\bigr)
\right\},
\label{eq:two_stage_value_form}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal B=\left\{B:
\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}B_d=B^{\mathrm{corp}},\quad
\underline B_d\le B_d\le\overline B_d\right\},
\end{equation*}
and $\Psi_{\overline T}$ is the network-constrained carbon-recourse value
function developed in Section~\ref{sec:carbon_recourse}.

Three institutional regimes are nested in the formulation.  Setting all
transfer capacities to zero gives independent depot accounts.  Positive
frictions and finite capacities give the network-constrained regime studied in
this paper.  If the transfer network is strongly connected, all transfer
frictions are zero, and capacities are sufficiently large never to bind, the
accounts can be rebalanced without friction and the model collapses to a fully
pooled corporate account.

The last regime also identifies a degeneracy.  Under frictionless uncapacitated
pooling, only the sum $\sum_dB_d=B^{\mathrm{corp}}$ affects recourse, so the
distribution of the first-stage budget may be non-unique and economically
irrelevant.  The allocation can likewise lose value when
$p^{\mathrm{buy}}=p^{\mathrm{sell}}$, or when every depot is always on the same
side of its budget so that no cross-depot netting opportunity arises.  The
first-stage decision is therefore economically active only when demand and
emission responsibilities are heterogeneous across depots and scenarios and
when internal transfers or external transactions are genuinely frictional.
These cases are limiting institutional regimes rather than the default
setting of the model; Corollary~\ref{cor:institutional_nesting} establishes
that their recourse costs are ordered.

\section{Structure of the Carbon Recourse}
\label{sec:carbon_recourse}
\label{subsec:structural}

This section isolates the accounting recourse that remains after a scenario
routing plan has fixed the depot emissions.  The resulting value function is
an exact component of the mixed-integer model, requires no convexification of
the routing decisions, and provides the marginal carbon signals used by the
subsequent solution method.  Convexity claims in this section concern the
continuous recourse function for fixed routing emissions; they do not imply
that the complete two-stage stochastic routing problem is convex.

\subsection{The carbon-recourse value function}
\label{subsec:recourse_value}

Suppress the scenario superscript and define the depot net-deficit vector
\begin{equation}
g_d=E_d-B_d,
\qquad d\in\mathcal D.
\label{eq:net_deficit}
\end{equation}
A positive $g_d$ is a deficit and a negative $g_d$ is a surplus.  For fixed
$g$, the minimum carbon-account recourse cost is
\begin{align}
\Psi_{\overline T}(g)=
\min_{t,b,s}\quad&
\sum_{(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T}\kappa_{dd'}t_{dd'}
+p^{\mathrm{buy}}\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}b_d
-p^{\mathrm{sell}}\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}s_d
\label{eq:recourse_primal}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&
 b_d+\sum_{(h,d)\in\delta_T^-(d)}t_{hd}
 -\sum_{(d,h)\in\delta_T^+(d)}t_{dh}-s_d=g_d,
&&\forall d\in\mathcal D,
\label{eq:recourse_balance}\\
&0\le t_{dd'}\le\overline T_{dd'},
&&\forall(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T,
\label{eq:recourse_capacity}\\
&b_d,s_d\ge0,
&&\forall d\in\mathcal D.
\label{eq:recourse_domain}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Complete recourse and minimum-cost-flow structure]
\label{prop:recourse_flow}
For every $g\in\mathbb R^{|\mathcal D|}$, problem
\eqref{eq:recourse_primal}--\eqref{eq:recourse_domain} is feasible and has a
finite optimum.  It is a capacitated minimum-cost transshipment problem on the
internal transfer network augmented by an external-market node.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Feasibility follows by setting $t=0$, $b_d=[g_d]_+$, and
$s_d=[-g_d]_+$, where $[u]_+=\max\{u,0\}$.  To obtain the network
interpretation, add an external-market node.  A purchase is an arc from the
market node to depot $d$ with unit cost $p^{\mathrm{buy}}$, a sale is an arc
from depot $d$ to the market node with unit cost $-p^{\mathrm{sell}}$, and an
internal arc $(d,d')$ has cost $\kappa_{dd'}$ and capacity
$\overline T_{dd'}$.  The depot imbalances are $g$.  Internal cycles have
nonnegative cost, and every cycle that purchases externally and later sells
externally has cost at least
$p^{\mathrm{buy}}-p^{\mathrm{sell}}>0$.  Hence no negative-cost cycle can
create unbounded arbitrage, and the feasible transshipment problem has a
finite optimum.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[No simultaneous external purchase and sale]
\label{lem:no_simultaneous_trade}
At an optimal solution of \eqref{eq:recourse_primal}, a depot cannot satisfy
$b_d>0$ and $s_d>0$ simultaneously.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If both variables are positive, decrease them by
$\varepsilon=\min\{b_d,s_d\}>0$.  The balance constraint is unchanged, whereas
the objective decreases by
$(p^{\mathrm{buy}}-p^{\mathrm{sell}})\varepsilon>0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Dual representation and depot prices}
\label{subsec:price_polyhedron}

Associate a free multiplier $\lambda_d$ with the balance equation of depot
$d$ and a nonnegative multiplier $\mu_{dd'}$ with the upper bound on transfer
arc $(d,d')$.  The dual of \eqref{eq:recourse_primal} is
\begin{align}
\Psi_{\overline T}(g)=
\max_{\lambda,\mu}\quad&
\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}\lambda_d g_d
-\sum_{(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T}
 \overline T_{dd'}\mu_{dd'}
\label{eq:recourse_dual}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&
p^{\mathrm{sell}}\le\lambda_d\le p^{\mathrm{buy}},
&&\forall d\in\mathcal D,
\label{eq:dual_price_bounds}\\
&\lambda_{d'}-\lambda_d-\mu_{dd'}\le\kappa_{dd'},
&&\forall(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T,
\label{eq:dual_transfer_arbitrage}\\
&\mu_{dd'}\ge0,
&&\forall(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T.
\label{eq:dual_mu_domain}
\end{align}
Define the extended price polyhedron
\begin{equation}
\widetilde\Lambda=
\left\{(\lambda,\mu):
\eqref{eq:dual_price_bounds}--\eqref{eq:dual_mu_domain}\right\}.
\label{eq:extended_price_polyhedron}
\end{equation}
The variable $\lambda_d$ is the marginal cost of an additional unit of
emission at depot $d$, equivalently the marginal saving from one additional
unit of budget at that depot while the routing emissions are fixed.  The
external market bounds this value between its sale and purchase prices.  The
arc inequality prevents a price difference from exceeding the cost of moving
one unit of internal credit, unless the transfer capacity is congested and
therefore carries a positive multiplier $\mu_{dd'}$.

\begin{proposition}[Price-polyhedral dual representation]
\label{prop:price_polyhedron}
For every $g$, strong linear-programming duality gives
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\overline T}(g)=
\max_{(\lambda,\mu)\in\widetilde\Lambda}
\left\{
\lambda^\top g-\sum_{(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T}
\overline T_{dd'}\mu_{dd'}
\right\}.
\label{eq:extended_support_representation}
\end{equation}
If the model omits transfer capacities (or one restricts attention to a
region in which they are known not to bind), the capacity multipliers can be
omitted and
\begin{equation}
\Psi_\infty(g)=\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^\top g,
\label{eq:price_support_representation}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\Lambda=
\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb R^{|\mathcal D|}:
 p^{\mathrm{sell}}\le\lambda_d\le p^{\mathrm{buy}},\quad
 \lambda_{d'}-\lambda_d\le\kappa_{dd'}
 \ \forall(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T
\right\}.
\label{eq:price_polyhedron}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write each balance equation as
$A(t,b,s)=g$.  For the minimisation problem, the equality multipliers are free.
The purchase column is $e_d$, giving $\lambda_d\le p^{\mathrm{buy}}$; the sale
column is $-e_d$, giving $-\lambda_d\le-p^{\mathrm{sell}}$; and the transfer
column for $(d,d')$ is $-e_d+e_{d'}$.  Introducing
$\mu_{dd'}\ge0$ for $t_{dd'}\le\overline T_{dd'}$ gives
$\lambda_{d'}-\lambda_d-\mu_{dd'}\le\kappa_{dd'}$ and contributes
$-\overline T_{dd'}\mu_{dd'}$ to the dual objective.  Proposition
\ref{prop:recourse_flow} supplies primal feasibility and boundedness, so strong
duality yields \eqref{eq:extended_support_representation}.  Removing the
upper bounds eliminates $\mu$ and gives \eqref{eq:price_support_representation}
and \eqref{eq:price_polyhedron}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Shape and monotonicity of the recourse value]
\label{prop:recourse_properties}
The function $\Psi_{\overline T}:\mathbb R^{|\mathcal D|}\to\mathbb R$ is
finite, convex, continuous, and piecewise linear.  It is componentwise
nondecreasing.  In the uncapacitated case it is the support function of the
compact price polyhedron $\Lambda$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Finiteness follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:recourse_flow}.  Representation
\eqref{eq:extended_support_representation} expresses the value as the pointwise
maximum of affine functions of $g$, which is convex and piecewise linear; a
finite polyhedral convex function is continuous on all of
$\mathbb R^{|\mathcal D|}$.  Every dual-feasible price satisfies
$\lambda_d\ge p^{\mathrm{sell}}\ge0$, so increasing one component of $g$ cannot
decrease any affine function in the representation and therefore cannot
decrease $\Psi_{\overline T}$.  When capacities are absent,
\eqref{eq:price_support_representation} is precisely the support function of
$\Lambda$, which is compact because all price components are bounded.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Complementarity and congestion}

\begin{proposition}[Complementary price conditions]
\label{prop:complementarity}
Let $(t^*,b^*,s^*)$ and $(\lambda^*,\mu^*)$ be primal and dual optimal for a
given $g$.  Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item if $b_d^*>0$, then $\lambda_d^*=p^{\mathrm{buy}}$;
\item if $s_d^*>0$, then $\lambda_d^*=p^{\mathrm{sell}}$;
\item if $t_{dd'}^*=0$ and $\overline T_{dd'}>0$, then
      $\lambda_{d'}^*-\lambda_d^*\le\kappa_{dd'}$;
\item if $0<t_{dd'}^*<\overline T_{dd'}$, then
      $\lambda_{d'}^*-\lambda_d^*=\kappa_{dd'}$;
\item if $t_{dd'}^*=\overline T_{dd'}>0$, then
      $\lambda_{d'}^*-\lambda_d^*=\kappa_{dd'}+\mu_{dd'}^*$, where
      $\mu_{dd'}^*\ge0$ is the congestion value of one additional unit of
      transfer capacity.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first two statements follow from complementary slackness for the purchase
and sale columns.  For transfer arc $(d,d')$, complementary slackness gives
\begin{equation*}
t_{dd'}^*\left[
\kappa_{dd'}-(\lambda_{d'}^*-\lambda_d^*)+\mu_{dd'}^*
\right]=0,
\qquad
\mu_{dd'}^*(\overline T_{dd'}-t_{dd'}^*)=0.
\end{equation*}
If the arc is unused and has positive residual capacity, the second equation
implies $\mu_{dd'}^*=0$ and dual feasibility gives the inequality.  If the
flow is strictly between its bounds, both complementary relations force
$\mu_{dd'}^*=0$ and equality with the friction cost.  If the positive flow is
at capacity, the reduced-cost condition is tight and the remaining multiplier
appears as stated.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Network bound on depot price differences]
\label{cor:shortest_path_price}
In the uncapacitated model, for every directed transfer path $P$ from depot
$u$ to depot $v$,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_v-\lambda_u\le\sum_{(d,d')\in P}\kappa_{dd'}.
\label{eq:path_price_bound}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_v-\lambda_u\le\operatorname{dist}_\kappa(u,v),
\label{eq:shortest_path_price_bound}
\end{equation}
where the right-hand side is the minimum directed transfer cost and is
$+\infty$ if no directed path exists.  With finite capacities, the same
argument holds at an optimal dual solution with effective arc lengths
$\kappa_{dd'}+\mu_{dd'}^*$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Sum the arc inequalities in \eqref{eq:price_polyhedron} along $P$; all
intermediate prices telescope.  Minimising the resulting right-hand side over
paths gives \eqref{eq:shortest_path_price_bound}.  Under finite capacities,
Proposition~\ref{prop:complementarity} and
\eqref{eq:dual_transfer_arbitrage} replace each friction by its friction plus
congestion multiplier.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Common price under frictionless pooling]
\label{cor:common_price}
If the transfer network is strongly connected, every transfer friction is
zero, and capacities are absent or nonbinding, then every feasible price in
$\Lambda$ satisfies
\begin{equation*}
\lambda_d=\lambda_{d'}
\qquad\forall d,d'\in\mathcal D.
\end{equation*}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For every pair of depots there is a zero-cost directed path in each direction.
Corollary~\ref{cor:shortest_path_price} gives both
$\lambda_{d'}-\lambda_d\le0$ and $\lambda_d-\lambda_{d'}\le0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Subgradients and institutional regimes}
\label{subsec:recourse_subgradient}

\begin{proposition}[Optimal depot prices are recourse subgradients]
\label{prop:recourse_subgradient}
Let $(\lambda^*,\mu^*)$ solve the dual at $g$.  Then
\begin{equation}
\lambda^*\in\partial\Psi_{\overline T}(g),
\label{eq:recourse_subgradient}
\end{equation}
that is, for every $g'$,
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\overline T}(g')\ge
\Psi_{\overline T}(g)+(\lambda^*)^\top(g'-g).
\label{eq:subgradient_inequality}
\end{equation}
Thus $\lambda_d^*$ is simultaneously the local marginal recourse cost of one
additional unit of depot emission and the local marginal saving from one
additional unit of depot budget, conditional on the current routing emissions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Dual feasibility of $(\lambda^*,\mu^*)$ at every right-hand side gives
\begin{align*}
\Psi_{\overline T}(g')
&\ge (\lambda^*)^\top g'
-\sum_{(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T}\overline T_{dd'}\mu_{dd'}^*\\
&=\Psi_{\overline T}(g)+(\lambda^*)^\top(g'-g),
\end{align*}
where the equality uses optimality at $g$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Scope of the marginal signal]
\label{rem:local_price_scope}
The subgradient in Proposition~\ref{prop:recourse_subgradient} is exact for the
continuous carbon-recourse function with the routing emissions fixed.  The
complete scenario value
\begin{equation*}
Q(B,\omega)=\min_{S\in\mathcal X(q^\omega)}
\left\{C_{\mathrm{op}}(S)+\Psi_{\overline T}(E(S)-B)\right\}
\end{equation*}
contains integer routing decisions and need not be convex in $B$.  A depot
price obtained at the current routing plan is therefore a rigorous recourse
subgradient and a structurally meaningful local search signal, but it is not
claimed to be a global subgradient of the full stochastic integer value
function.
\end{remark}

Define
\begin{equation}
\varphi(u)=\max\{p^{\mathrm{buy}}u,\ p^{\mathrm{sell}}u\}.
\label{eq:single_account_settlement}
\end{equation}
The following special cases connect the network-constrained model to its
institutional regimes.

\begin{proposition}[Independent and fully pooled special cases]
\label{prop:institutional_cases}
For a fixed net-deficit vector $g$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item if every transfer capacity is zero, then
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\mathrm{ind}}(g)=\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}\varphi(g_d);
\label{eq:independent_recourse}
\end{equation}
\item if the transfer network is strongly connected, all frictions are zero,
      and capacities are absent or nonbinding, then
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\mathrm{pool}}(g)=\varphi\!\left(\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}g_d\right).
\label{eq:pooled_recourse}
\end{equation}
In the fully pooled case, $\Psi_{\mathrm{pool}}(E-B)$ depends on $B$ only
through $\sum_dB_d=B^{\mathrm{corp}}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Without transfers, each balance equation is independent and the optimal
purchase--sale cost of net position $g_d$ is $\varphi(g_d)$.  Under
frictionless uncapacitated strong connectivity, Corollary
\ref{cor:common_price} restricts the price polyhedron to vectors
$\lambda=\alpha\mathbf 1$ with
$\alpha\in[p^{\mathrm{sell}},p^{\mathrm{buy}}]$.  The support representation
then becomes
\begin{equation*}
\max_{\alpha\in[p^{\mathrm{sell}},p^{\mathrm{buy}}]}
\alpha\sum_dg_d
=\varphi\!\left(\sum_dg_d\right).
\end{equation*}
Since $\sum_d(E_d-B_d)=\sum_dE_d-B^{\mathrm{corp}}$, the distribution of the
first-stage budget does not affect pooled recourse.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Canonical implementation of the fully pooled regime]
\label{rem:canonical_pooling}
The fully pooled regime should be evaluated directly through
\eqref{eq:pooled_recourse}, or equivalently by a single corporate market
account.  Implementing it as a strongly connected zero-cost transfer network
can create arbitrary zero-cost circulation flows: the recourse value and the
common price remain correct, but the resulting arc flows are non-unique and
have no managerial interpretation.  If depot-to-depot flows must be displayed,
a secondary optimisation should minimise $\sum_{(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T}t_{dd'}$
over the primary optimal face, or an acyclic canonical transfer representation
should be imposed.  Transfer-volume statistics from an arbitrary zero-cost
optimal flow are not reported.
\end{remark}

The three regimes described informally in
Section~\ref{subsec:model_interpretation} can now be ranked.

\begin{corollary}[Institutional nesting]
\label{cor:institutional_nesting}
Consider otherwise identical routing and budget-allocation models.  Let the
fully pooled regime have zero transfer frictions and nonbinding capacities,
let the network-constrained regime use the specified positive frictions and
finite capacities, and let the independent regime prohibit all internal
transfers.  Then
\begin{equation}
Z_{\mathrm{full\ pool}}^*
\le Z_{\mathrm{network}}^*
\le Z_{\mathrm{independent}}^*.
\label{eq:institutional_nesting}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Moving from independent accounts to the network-constrained regime adds
feasible transfer decisions, and moving from the network-constrained regime
to frictionless uncapacitated pooling weakly enlarges the transfer feasible set
and weakly decreases transfer costs.  Every routing and budget plan feasible
under a more restrictive regime remains feasible under the less restrictive
regime, so minimising over the nested feasible sets gives the inequalities.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Degenerate budget-allocation cases}
The special cases also delimit the economic scope of the first stage.  Under
frictionless full pooling, any feasible allocation with the same corporate
total produces the same recourse value.  If
$p^{\mathrm{buy}}=p^{\mathrm{sell}}$, the external settlement is linear and
cross-depot netting no longer avoids a bid--ask spread.  Finally, if all depots
are simultaneously in deficit in every relevant scenario, the optimal
external purchase cost is
$p^{\mathrm{buy}}(\sum_dE_d-B^{\mathrm{corp}})$; if all are simultaneously in
surplus, the corresponding sale value depends only on the same aggregate
position.  In either case, the distribution of $B$ may cease to affect the
accounting cost.  Meaningful ex ante allocation therefore requires
cross-depot and cross-scenario heterogeneity together with nontrivial internal
or external frictions.



Two consequences of this structure are used by the solution method of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.  First, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:recourse_subgradient} the optimal price vector is a
subgradient of $\Psi_{\overline T}$ at the current deficit, so the
price-linearised change in the objective produced by a routing move is a
valid lower bound on its true change. A move that improves the true cost
therefore improves the linearisation as well, and a search conducted on the
linearised objective cannot overlook an improvement of the exact one.  Second, $\Psi_{\overline T}$ is convex and piecewise linear in
its argument, hence convex in the budget $B$ once route emissions are fixed,
so the first-stage allocation can be re-optimised exactly by linear
programming rather than heuristically.  The remaining difficulty is the
integer routing problem, which none of these results convexifies.

\section{Solution Method}
\label{sec:methodology}

The deterministic equivalent in Section~\ref{sec:problem_formulation} is exact but grows rapidly with the number of demand scenarios and routing variables. We therefore develop the \emph{price-polyhedral budget--routing coordination} matheuristic (PPBRC). Its design follows directly from the recourse value function in Section~\ref{sec:carbon_recourse}. The method alternates between scenario-wise routing optimisation at a fixed first-stage budget and an exact multi-scenario budget--recourse optimisation at fixed route emissions. The first block uses depot prices and transfer-capacity multipliers generated by the exact carbon-recourse oracle; the second block reallocates the corporate carbon budget without approximating the accounting recourse.

\subsection{Solution framework}
\label{subsec:ppbrc_architecture}
\label{subsec:ppbrc_overall}

A solution consists of a first-stage vector $B\in\mathcal B$ and, for every
scenario $\omega\in\mathcal W$, a feasible routing plan $S^\omega$. Writing
$C_{\mathrm{op}}^\omega(S^\omega)$ for the dispatch and travel cost and
$E^\omega(S^\omega)$ for the vector of depot emissions, the cost of scenario
$\omega$ is
\begin{equation}
 Z_\omega(S^\omega;B)
 =C_{\mathrm{op}}^\omega(S^\omega)
 +\Psi_{\overline T}\!\left(E^\omega(S^\omega)-B\right),
 \label{eq:ppbrc_true_scenario}
\end{equation}
and the objective of the model is
$Z(B,S)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal W}\pi_\omega Z_\omega(S^\omega;B)$.

The formulation decomposes in two directions. Fixing $B$ removes the only
variable common to the scenarios, so the second-stage problems separate and
may be treated independently. Fixing the emissions $E^\omega$ removes the
combinatorial content, leaving a linear programme in the budget and the
accounting variables. Neither restriction is a relaxation: each is an exact
subproblem of the deterministic equivalent, and the method alternates between
them.

The two subproblems differ in tractability, and the method treats them
differently for that reason. The routing subproblem is $\mathcal{NP}$-hard by
Proposition~\ref{prop:complexity} and is addressed heuristically. The budget
subproblem is convex by Proposition~\ref{prop:recourse_properties} and is
solved to optimality by linear programming. The price polyhedron of
Section~\ref{sec:carbon_recourse} supplies the link between them: it
expresses the accounting consequence of a change in emissions as a vector of
depot prices, which is precisely the information the routing subproblem
requires and could otherwise obtain only by re-solving a recourse problem for
each candidate. The prices themselves are defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:recourse_oracle}.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ppbrc} states the resulting procedure, and
Figure~\ref{fig:ppbrc_flow} shows its structure.

An initial routing plan is obtained for each scenario by inserting customers at their cheapest feasible position, opening a vehicle when no such position exists. The initial budget is proportional to expected reference emissions and is projected onto $\mathcal B$. Two variants are distinguished. \emph{PPBRC-core} runs the scheme from a single deterministic construction and confines the neighbourhood search to small removals. \emph{PPBRC} constructs several initial plans and retains the best, and admits occasional large removals during the search. The distinction isolates the contribution of the coordination scheme from that of diversification, and both variants are reported separately in Section~\ref{sec:computational_experiments}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Price-Polyhedral Budget--Routing Coordination (PPBRC)}
\label{alg:ppbrc}
\KwIn{Scenario set $\mathcal W$ and probabilities $\pi$; routing data; $B^{\mathrm{corp}}$; transfer network $(\mathcal D,\mathcal A^T)$, costs $\kappa$, and capacities $\overline T$.}
\KwOut{Budget $B^{\mathrm{best}}$, scenario routes $S^{\omega,\mathrm{best}}$, and recourse decisions.}
Initialise $B^0$ proportionally to expected reference emissions and project onto $\mathcal B$\;
\For{$r\leftarrow0,1,\ldots,R-1$}{
  \ForEach{$\omega\in\mathcal W$ \textbf{\emph{(independent, parallelisable)}}}{
    Initialise $\lambda^\omega$ at the midpoint of $[p^{\mathrm{sell}},p^{\mathrm{buy}}]$\;
    \For{$\ell\leftarrow1,\ldots,L$}{
      Improve $S^\omega$ by adaptive large neighbourhood search on
      $C_{\mathrm{op}}^\omega(S^\omega)+(\lambda^\omega)^\top E^\omega(S^\omega)$\;
      Solve the recourse dual at $g^\omega=E^\omega(S^\omega)-B^r$ and update $\lambda^\omega$\;
      \lIf{$\lambda^\omega$ is unchanged}{\textbf{break}}
    }
  }
  Fix $E^{\omega}$ for all $\omega$ and solve \eqref{eq:budget_block_obj}--\eqref{eq:budget_block_domain} exactly for $B^{r+1}$\;
  \lIf{$\|B^{r+1}-B^r\|_1$ is negligible}{\textbf{break}}
}
Evaluate $Z(B,S)$ with exact recourse under the complete objective \eqref{eq:ppbrc_true_scenario}\;
\Return{$B$, $\{S^\omega\}$, and their exact recourse decisions}\;
\end{algorithm}

Two loops are nested inside the outer coordination loop. The inner loop over
$\ell$ is the price fixed point of Section~\ref{subsec:price_neighbourhoods}:
routing and price are alternated at a fixed budget until the price stabilises.
The outer loop over $r$ is the coordination itself, in which the budget is
re-optimised exactly once scenario emissions are known. PPBRC-core denotes
this scheme with a single deterministic start and no diversification;
complete PPBRC adds a multi-start construction and periodic large removals
inside the neighbourhood search, and is the variant used unless stated
otherwise.

\subsection{The scenario routing subproblem}
\label{subsec:recourse_oracle}
\label{subsec:price_neighbourhoods}

The prices on which this subproblem depends are defined first.

Let $g^\omega=E^\omega(S^\omega)-B$ denote the deficit induced by a pair
$(B,S^\omega)$. Solving \eqref{eq:recourse_primal} and its dual
\eqref{eq:recourse_dual} yields a recourse flow $(t^\omega,b^\omega,s^\omega)$
and a dual pair $(\lambda^\omega,\mu^\omega)$, in which $\lambda_d^\omega$ is
the marginal cost of emission at depot $d$ and $\mu_{dd'}^\omega$ the marginal
value of capacity on transfer arc $(d,d')$. When the optimal flow is not
unique, minimising total internal transfer over the optimal face selects a
canonical representative; this leaves $\Psi_{\overline T}(g^\omega)$ unchanged
and makes reported transfer volumes well defined. The fully pooled regime is
evaluated through Remark~\ref{rem:canonical_pooling}.

Consider a modification $m$ of a routing plan, with cost change
$\Delta C_m^\omega$ and emission change $\Delta E_m^\omega$, and define
\begin{equation}
 L_m^\omega
 :=\Delta C_m^\omega+(\lambda^\omega)^\top\Delta E_m^\omega .
 \label{eq:move_lower_bound}
\end{equation}
Proposition~\ref{prop:recourse_subgradient} and the convexity of
$\Psi_{\overline T}$ give
\begin{equation}
 \Delta Z_m^\omega\ \ge\ L_m^\omega ,
 \label{eq:move_subgradient_bound}
\end{equation}
so that $L_m^\omega$ underestimates the true change in cost. No modification
with $L_m^\omega\ge0$ can improve the scenario objective. The converse fails:
a negative value identifies a candidate, not an improvement.

For a fixed price vector $\lambda^\omega$, the routing subproblem of scenario
$\omega$ is
\begin{equation}
 \min_{S^\omega\in\mathcal S^\omega}\;
 C_{\mathrm{op}}^\omega(S^\omega)+(\lambda^\omega)^\top E^\omega(S^\omega),
 \label{eq:priced_routing_subproblem}
\end{equation}
a multi-depot routing problem with capacity, route-duration and eligibility
restrictions in which emission carries a linear charge that differs by depot.
Its objective is the linearisation whose increments are the quantities
\eqref{eq:move_lower_bound}. By \eqref{eq:move_subgradient_bound}, any
modification that reduces the true scenario cost also reduces this
linearisation, so searching over \eqref{eq:priced_routing_subproblem}
overlooks no improvement of the true cost; the converse fails, and a
modification that improves the linearisation need not improve the true
cost, which is why solutions are re-evaluated exactly.

Problem \eqref{eq:priced_routing_subproblem} is solved by adaptive large
neighbourhood search \citep{ropke2006adaptive,pisinger2007general}. The
removal operators follow the structure of the priced objective. Removing
customers at random preserves diversification. Removing those whose priced
contribution is largest directs the search towards emission-intensive
customers where the depot price is high and towards distant ones where it is
low, so that a single operator adapts to the prevailing carbon position of
each depot. Removing geographically related customers allows a coherent
portion of a route to be rebuilt. Removing customers with more than one
eligible depot addresses the reassignment specific to this problem: such
customers are the only ones whose service can be moved between carbon
accounts, and a search that reaches them only incidentally examines few of
the reassignments that the price signal makes attractive. Reinsertion uses a
cheapest-feasible-position rule and a regret rule, the latter being material
here because a customer eligible at two depots with comparable insertion
costs but different prices should not be committed to the cheaper position
before the alternative has been evaluated. Acceptance follows a
simulated-annealing criterion, and every insertion is verified against
capacity, route duration, fleet availability and depot eligibility.

The price is not fixed throughout. Once the search terminates, the recourse
dual is re-solved at the resulting emissions and returns an updated
$\lambda^\omega$, and the search is repeated. This alternation is a
fixed-point iteration in the price, terminating when $\lambda^\omega$ is
unchanged or after a prescribed number of repetitions. Solutions are
evaluated for reporting and comparison by the complete cost
\eqref{eq:ppbrc_true_scenario} with exact recourse; the linearised objective
governs only the search carried out at a fixed price.

\subsection{The budget subproblem}
\label{subsec:budget_recourse_block}

Suppose the route emissions $\widehat E_d^\omega$ are fixed for every scenario. The first-stage budget and the accounting decisions of all scenarios are then determined by the linear programme
\begin{align}
 \min_{B,t,b,s}\quad
 &\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal W}\pi_\omega\left[
 \sum_{(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T}\kappa_{dd'}t_{dd'}^\omega
 +p^{\mathrm{buy}}\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}b_d^\omega
 -p^{\mathrm{sell}}\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}s_d^\omega\right]
 \label{eq:budget_block_obj}\\
 \text{s.t.}\quad
 &B_d+\sum_{(h,d)\in\delta_T^-(d)}t_{hd}^\omega+b_d^\omega
 =\widehat E_d^\omega+\sum_{(d,h)\in\delta_T^+(d)}t_{dh}^\omega+s_d^\omega,
 &&\forall d,\omega,\label{eq:budget_block_balance}\\
 &\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}B_d=B^{\mathrm{corp}},\qquad
 \underline B_d\le B_d\le\overline B_d,
 &&\forall d,\label{eq:budget_block_budget}\\
 &0\le t_{dd'}^\omega\le\overline T_{dd'},
 &&\forall(d,d')\in\mathcal A^T,\ \forall\omega,\label{eq:budget_block_transfer}\\
 &b_d^\omega,s_d^\omega\ge0,
 &&\forall d,\omega.\label{eq:budget_block_domain}
\end{align}
The operational cost is constant here and is omitted from \eqref{eq:budget_block_obj}. If several budgets attain the optimum, any of them may be returned; the optimal value, which is what enters the objective, is unaffected.

Let $\nu$ be the multiplier of the corporate budget equality, and let $\alpha_d^L,\alpha_d^U\ge0$ be the multipliers of the lower and upper budget bounds. With the sign convention of Section~\ref{subsec:price_polyhedron}, the KKT condition for $B_d$ is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\omega\in\mathcal W}\pi_\omega\lambda_d^\omega
 =\nu-\alpha_d^L+\alpha_d^U.
 \label{eq:expected_price_kkt}
\end{equation}
Every depot whose budget lies strictly between its bounds therefore carries the same expected marginal carbon value, while a depot at its lower or upper bound satisfies the corresponding one-sided condition.

\subsection{Properties and complexity}
\label{subsec:ppbrc_properties}

The method is heuristic in its routing component and exact in its budget
component. The following statement makes the second half precise, and is the
only optimality guarantee the method carries.

\begin{proposition}[Exactness of the budget step]
\label{prop:ppbrc_budget_exact}
Fix scenario emissions $\widehat E^\omega$ for all $\omega\in\mathcal W$.
Then the optimal value of
\eqref{eq:budget_block_obj}--\eqref{eq:budget_block_domain} equals
\begin{equation*}
\min_{B\in\mathcal B}\ \sum_{\omega\in\mathcal W}\pi_\omega
\Psi_{\overline T}\!\left(\widehat E^\omega-B\right),
\end{equation*}
so the budget step returns a first-stage allocation that is globally optimal
for those emissions, and the expected cost after the step is no larger than
before it.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For each $\omega$, $\Psi_{\overline T}(\widehat E^\omega-B)$ is by definition
the optimal value of the recourse problem
\eqref{eq:recourse_primal}--\eqref{eq:recourse_domain} at deficit
$\widehat E^\omega-B$. Replacing each $\Psi_{\overline T}$ term by that
problem and minimising jointly over $B$ and the recourse variables of all
scenarios yields exactly
\eqref{eq:budget_block_obj}--\eqref{eq:budget_block_domain}, since the
scenarios share no variable other than $B$ and the operating cost is constant
at fixed emissions. The joint minimisation is a linear programme and is
solved to optimality. Finally, the budget in force before the step, together
with its recourse decisions, is feasible for that programme, so its value is
an upper bound on the optimum.
\end{proof}

No comparable guarantee is available for the routing component, which
addresses an $\mathcal{NP}$-hard problem
(Proposition~\ref{prop:complexity}) by neighbourhood search under a
simulated-annealing acceptance rule. The expected cost is therefore not
monotone along the iterations, and neither convergence of the iterates nor
local optimality at termination is claimed. What can be said is that every
reported solution is feasible and is evaluated under the complete objective
\eqref{eq:ppbrc_true_scenario} with exact recourse, so reported costs are
never those of the linearised surrogate used inside the search. The
computational study in Section~\ref{sec:computational_experiments} assesses
solution quality against exact optima on instances small enough to admit
them, and against benchmark methods otherwise.

\paragraph{Computational considerations}

The accounting components are small. For one scenario the recourse problem
has $|\mathcal D|$ balance constraints, $|\mathcal A^T|$ internal arcs and
$2|\mathcal D|$ market arcs, and is a capacitated minimum-cost flow problem.
The budget problem has $|\mathcal D|$ shared variables and, per scenario,
$|\mathcal A^T|+2|\mathcal D|$ accounting variables subject to
$|\mathcal D|$ balance constraints, so its size grows linearly in
$|\mathcal W|(|\mathcal A^T|+|\mathcal D|)$. The computational effort is
dominated by the routing search rather than by either linear programme.

Two structural features govern the cost of that search. Distances are
precomputed, and only the routes affected by a candidate modification are
re-evaluated, so the load-dependent emission of an unaffected route is never
recomputed. At a fixed budget the scenario routing subproblems share no
variable and may therefore be solved in parallel, with synchronisation
required only before the budget step; the computational study reports this
structural independence separately from realised speedup, which depends on
the execution environment.

algorithm with external benchmark methods and attributes any advantage to its

the algorithm to the model, and asks whether treating the budget allocation
as a stochastic decision is worthwhile at all, relative to a deterministic
expected-value allocation and to simple managerial rules; this is measured by
the value of the stochastic solution and the expected value of perfect
information.  Section~\ref{subsec:e4_recourse_interaction} asks a different
question -- not whether the model is worthwhile, but under which operating
conditions its two recourse channels are complementary or substitutable --
and answers it with a replicated factorial design over the demand and
transfer-network environment.  Section~\ref{subsec:e5_public_benchmark}
repeats the routing and mechanism tests on public benchmark instances, whose
spatial and capacity structure is not of our construction.
Section~\ref{subsec:claims_evidence} summarises what the five experiments
jointly establish, and what they do not.

The algorithm architecture, benchmark methods, instance classes, scenario
streams, and search seeds are all fixed before any reported run.

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{subsec:experimental_setup}

\subsubsection{Test instances}
\label{subsubsec:test_instances}

Two datasets are used, each playing a role standard in this literature: a numerical-experiment dataset for controlled comparison, and a case-study dataset for external validity.  The numerical-experiment dataset comprises 94 reported instances (30 verification, 24 comparison, 8 stochastic-value, 32 sensitivity; Table~\ref{tab:frozen_data_suites}), together with 8 further instances used only for parameter calibration, all produced by a single instance-generation procedure that follows the classical multi-depot geometry of \citet{cordeau1997vrp}, the controlled clustered-instance methodology of \citet{uchoa2017benchmark}, the correlated-demand-shock decomposition surveyed by \citet{gendreau1996stochastic}, and the load-dependent fuel-consumption model of \citet{bektas2011pollution}.  Depot placement, core/boundary customer sampling, demand-shock generation, reference-emission construction, and transfer-network assembly are identical across every suite; the suites differ only in the scale and factor settings supplied to this procedure -- customer and depot counts, scenario-bank sizes, and, for E2 and E4, the carbon-network stratum or factorial cell -- as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:frozen_data_suites} and Appendix~\ref{app:scenario_generation}.

These differences follow directly from what each suite is designed to isolate, not from ad hoc regeneration.  The verification suite is kept small enough for exact solution by a mixed-integer solver; the comparison suite spans a size range wide enough to separate heuristic performance; the sensitivity suite fixes instance size entirely so that its $2^4$ factorial design attributes performance differences to the four manipulated factors rather than to incidental geometry changes.  Its 32 instances are the 16 factorial cells replicated twice, a count determined by the design rather than chosen for convenience.  All instance and scenario parameters are fixed in advance of algorithm comparison and are not revised after performance is observed.  Algorithm parameters -- neighbourhood limits, stopping rules, and wall-clock budgets -- are calibrated on the 8 separate instances mentioned above, which are generated by the same procedure but are disjoint from every instance on which results are reported; no parameter is tuned on an instance whose performance is subsequently reported.

The case-study dataset is the public Cordeau multi-depot benchmark itself \citep{cordeau1997vrp} (33 instances, used without modification), which Section~\ref{subsec:e5_public_benchmark} uses to test the routing engine against a recognised external benchmark (E5a) and to run the complete carbon-budget mechanism on a real, rather than self-generated, spatial and capacity structure (E5b); that section states precisely what each of these two roles does and does not establish.

Depot locations are placed on a mildly perturbed regular polygon.  Core customers are sampled around one home depot, whereas a pre-specified fraction of boundary customers is sampled around the midpoint of adjacent depots and is eligible for both.  Let $\bar q_i$ denote the base demand of customer $i$.  Scenario demands follow
\begin{equation}
 q_i^\omega
 =\bar q_i
 \exp\!\left(\sigma Z_{h(i)}^\omega-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)
 \exp\!\left(\sigma_\varepsilon\varepsilon_i^\omega-
                  \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{2}\right),
 \label{eq:scenario_generator}
\end{equation}
where $Z^\omega$ is a depot-level Gaussian factor with equicorrelation $\rho$, and $\varepsilon_i^\omega$ is an independent customer shock.  The lognormal correction preserves the base demand in expectation.  Extreme draws are truncated or resampled only to maintain positivity and individual-vehicle feasibility.  Negative cross-depot correlation is permitted whenever the equicorrelation matrix is positive semidefinite.

The corporate budget is parameterised as
\begin{equation}
 B^{\mathrm{corp}}=\gamma_B E^0,
 \label{eq:budget_stringency}
\end{equation}
where $E^0$ is obtained from a deterministic home-depot angle-sweep construction under base demand.  The reference allocation is proportional to depot reference emissions, with lower and upper responsibility bounds equal to $45\%$ and $160\%$ of that allocation.  The internal transfer graph is a bidirectional ring; the five-depot classes include two additional bidirectional chords.  Arc frictions are directionally asymmetric, and capacities are scaled relative to the reference emissions of their incident depots.

\subsubsection{Compared algorithms}
\label{subsubsec:algorithms_compared}

Six methods are compared.  Two are external benchmarks drawn from established
approaches to stochastic routing; three are restrictions of the proposed
method that disable one mechanism each, and serve to attribute performance to
individual components rather than to the architecture as a whole; the last is
the complete method.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{SAA-ALNS.} A sample-average-approximation benchmark: reactive destroy--repair search with random, worst, and related removals, greedy and regret-2 repair, simulated-annealing acceptance, and periodic exact optimisation of the shared budget.  It carries no price-polyhedral guidance.
\item \textbf{PH-ALNS.} A progressive-hedging benchmark with scenario-specific budget copies, exact recourse subgradients in projected proximal updates, adaptive penalty balancing, scenario-level ALNS, and exact feasible nonanticipative recovery.  Because progressive hedging is not exact for an integer routing problem, both its recovered feasible objective and its budget-consensus residual are reported.
\item \textbf{Cost-only coordination.} The proposed block architecture with depot prices and congestion multipliers removed.
\item \textbf{Price-guided routing only.} The price-polyhedral routing subproblem with the first-stage budget held at its initial value.
\item \textbf{PPBRC-core.} The single-trajectory price-polyhedral routing and exact budget-coordination scheme, without diversification.
\item \textbf{PPBRC.} PPBRC-core followed by bounded post-stagnation destroy--repair diversification and a final price-polyhedral refinement.
\end{enumerate}

Equal-allocation, expected-demand, and reference-emission budget rules are
additionally used in Section~\ref{subsec:e3_stochastic_value} as managerial
reference policies for the first-stage decision.

\subsubsection{Performance measures}
\label{subsubsec:performance_measures}

All methods are run under identical hardware, wall-clock limits, training
scenarios, deterministic reference starts, and the five pre-specified search
seeds $\{91001,\dots,91005\}$.  The benchmark methods receive the same exact
load-dependent route evaluator, carbon-recourse oracle, and shared-budget
feasibility recovery as the proposed method, so that differences in
performance reflect search and coordination rather than differences in
evaluation.  PPBRC-core and complete PPBRC receive the same total time limit, so that the
comparison between them isolates the effect of diversification at equal
computational effort.

Every reported solution is evaluated with the complete objective
\eqref{eq:ppbrc_true_scenario} and exact accounting recourse, never with the
surrogate used inside the search.  For each method we report the best and mean
objective over the search seeds, its standard deviation, the wall-clock time,
and the paired difference against each competing method on the same instance
and seed.

Statistical statements follow three rules.  Paired differences between methods
on identical instances and seeds are the primary evidence, and are reported
with their dispersion rather than in the mean alone.  Paired Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests at the $5\%$ level accompany them as descriptive summaries;
with 24 instances in the comparison suite such tests have limited power, so a
non-significant difference is reported as inconclusive rather than as evidence
of equality.  Where several methods are compared on the same suite, $p$-values
are Holm-corrected across the family of pairwise comparisons.  Factor effects
in Section~\ref{subsec:e4_recourse_interaction} are estimated from the
replicated factorial by least squares, with standard errors obtained from the
replicate variance.

All experiments are run on an Intel Core i5-1130G7 processor (four physical
cores, eight threads) with $16$~GB of RAM.  The implementation is in
Python~3.13 with NumPy~2.3, and every linear and mixed-integer programme --
the deterministic equivalent, the recourse problem and its dual, and the
budget master problem -- is solved with CBC through PuLP~3.3.  No commercial
solver is used, so the exact-solution results can be reproduced without a
licence.  The exact solver is allowed $1800$~s per instance in
Section~\ref{subsec:e1_verification}; in every other experiment all methods
receive an identical per-instance wall-clock budget, stated with that
experiment.  Because the scenario subproblems at a fixed budget are
independent, the implementation solves them in parallel, and all reported
times are wall-clock times for the complete run.

\subsection{Comparison with exact solutions}
\label{subsec:e1_verification}

Every instance of the verification suite is solved with the deterministic-equivalent formulation of Section~\ref{subsec:mathematical_formulation} under a $1800$~s limit.  The suite is deliberately small: in a preliminary run at $90$~s, optimality was proved only at $|\mathcal C|=10$ with few scenarios and never at $|\mathcal C|\ge15$, so a suite reaching further would report bounds rather than optima and could not support the comparison this section is for.  Sizes are therefore $|\mathcal C|\in\{8,12,16\}$ with at most six scenarios.  Where optimality is nonetheless not proved within the limit, the cell is reported as such rather than omitted, and the heuristic is assessed against the best valid bound; the proportion of cells in which a proven optimum is available is reported with the results, since it determines how much of the comparison rests on optima rather than on bounds. Before these main runs, tiny multi-vehicle instances are cross-checked with two independent exact formulations: the direct arc--payload deterministic-equivalent MILP in Section~\ref{subsec:mathematical_formulation}, and an exact route-plan extensive form obtained by enumerating every feasible customer assignment, vehicle partition, and route sequence in each scenario.  Agreement between these formulations isolates modelling and indexing errors that would not be detected by re-evaluating a heuristic solution with the same route code.

The verification script independently checks customer coverage, route continuity, the number of active vehicles, capacity, route duration, payload conservation, depot emissions, account balance, transfer capacity, first-stage non-anticipativity, and the absence of simultaneous buying and selling above tolerance. For every recourse call it also checks primal--dual equality, price bounds, and complementary conditions on unused, interior, and saturated transfer arcs.

\subsection{Value of stochastic budget allocation}
\label{subsec:e3_stochastic_value}

This experiment quantifies how much is gained by treating the first-stage
budget allocation as a stochastic decision rather than a deterministic one.
For each instance of the stochastic-value suite, the stochastic solution is
estimated from nested training samples of 20, 50, and 100 scenarios; because
the samples are nested prefixes of one bank, any change in performance is
attributable to sample size and not to a different random draw.  The training
sample size and the stopping settings are selected on the 200 independent
validation scenarios, and the selected policy is then evaluated once on the
800 sealed test scenarios.  The 200-scenario heavy-tailed regime-switching
bank is used only for the distribution-shift check reported in the appendix.

Six first-stage policies are compared: equal allocation, expected-demand
allocation, reference-emission allocation, the expected-value deterministic
solution, the stochastic solution, and a wait-and-see solution that may choose
$B$ after observing each scenario.  Let $Z_{\mathrm{EV}}$ denote the expected
cost of implementing the expected-value budget and reoptimising the second
stage, $Z_{\mathrm{SP}}$ the stochastic-programme value, and
$Z_{\mathrm{WS}}$ the wait-and-see value.  For this minimisation problem,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{VSS}=Z_{\mathrm{EV}}-Z_{\mathrm{SP}}\ge0,
 \qquad
 \mathrm{EVPI}=Z_{\mathrm{SP}}-Z_{\mathrm{WS}}\ge0.
 \label{eq:vss_evpi}
\end{equation}
The wait-and-see policy is an information bound, not an implementable policy;
it is reported to bound how much of the remaining cost is attributable to
demand uncertainty itself rather than to the allocation rule.

Two distinctions matter for interpretation.  First, VSS and EVPI are
in-sample quantities computed on the training bank, whereas the comparison of
implementable policies is made on the sealed test bank; an in-sample advantage
that does not survive on the test bank indicates overfitting to the scenario
sample rather than genuine value, and is reported as such.  Second, the
budget vectors and expected depot prices are reported alongside the cost
figures, since two allocations with similar cost may distribute carbon
responsibility very differently.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis of recourse interaction}
\label{subsec:e4_recourse_interaction}

The model provides two distinct recourse channels: operational recourse, which
reassigns boundary customers and reconstructs routes, and accounting recourse,
which moves carbon credit through the internal transfer network.  This
experiment measures the value of each channel, and of their joint use, and
establishes how that value depends on the operating environment.

Each instance is solved under four regimes obtained by enabling or disabling
the two channels.  Operational recourse is disabled by fixing every customer
to its home depot; accounting recourse is disabled by setting all internal
transfer capacities to zero.  External trading remains available throughout,
so that complete recourse is preserved and the four regimes remain comparable.
Let $Z_{ab}$ denote the expected cost, where $a$ and $b$ indicate whether
operational and accounting recourse are available.  Define
\begin{equation}
 V_{\mathrm{op}}=Z_{00}-Z_{10},\qquad
 V_{\mathrm{acc}}=Z_{00}-Z_{01},\qquad
 V_{\mathrm{joint}}=Z_{00}-Z_{11},
 \label{eq:recourse_values}
\end{equation}
with interaction
\begin{equation}
 I=V_{\mathrm{joint}}-V_{\mathrm{op}}-V_{\mathrm{acc}}.
 \label{eq:interaction_index}
\end{equation}
A positive $I$ indicates that the two channels are complementary relative to
additivity; a negative $I$ indicates that they substitute for one another.

The sensitivity suite varies four factors at two levels each in a replicated
$2^4$ design: the demand coefficient of variation, the cross-depot demand
correlation, the share of boundary customers, and the transfer-capacity scale
(Table~\ref{tab:parameter_values}).  Instance size is held fixed, so estimated
effects are attributable to these four factors rather than to incidental
differences in geometry.  Because the design is balanced and replicated, main
effects and two-factor interactions on $I$ are estimable without confounding,
which a fractional or unreplicated design would not permit.

The fully pooled regime is additionally reported as an institutional
benchmark.  Its recourse cost is computed directly from
\eqref{eq:pooled_recourse} rather than by assigning zero cost to a complete
transfer network, so that it yields aggregate external trading and a common
price without implying an arbitrary depot-to-depot transfer volume; see
Remark~\ref{rem:canonical_pooling}.

\subsection{Summary of the experimental evidence}
\label{subsec:claims_evidence}

Each experiment supports a specific and bounded claim.  The verification experiment establishes numerical consistency and small-instance solution quality, but cannot establish scalability.  The comparison experiment identifies the contribution of price-polyhedral coordination on the tested instances, but cannot prove dominance on all instances.  The stochastic-value experiment quantifies the value of stochastic optimisation under the chosen scenario model, and does not establish robustness to misspecified distributions.  The sensitivity experiment identifies empirical complementarity or substitution between the two recourse channels, and does not prove a universal sign for $I$.  Within the public-benchmark experiment, E5a establishes routing compatibility with a recognised MDVRP benchmark but does not validate the carbon-account extension, and E5b provides external spatial and capacity structure for the augmented model but does not inherit the original MDVRP best-known solutions, because its objective and information structure differ.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study introduces the CBAR-MDVRP, a two-stage stochastic routing model in
which headquarters delegates carbon budgets to depots before demand is
observed and coordinates two forms of recourse once it is realised. The
formulation separates physical recourse, through customer--depot reassignment
and route reconstruction, from accounting recourse, through a
capacity-constrained internal carbon-credit network and residual external
trading. The separation is what makes the first-stage allocation
economically meaningful: were credits transferable without friction after
demand is known, the allocation would be an accounting convention with no
operational content.

For fixed route emissions the carbon-account component is a capacitated
minimum-cost transshipment problem. Its dual defines an extended price
polyhedron whose depot prices measure marginal recourse costs and whose
capacity multipliers identify congested transfer channels. This yields exact
local information about the continuous recourse -- bounds on the prices,
complementarity conditions distinguishing unused, interior and saturated
channels, and a subgradient inequality bounding the effect of a routing
change from below -- without convexifying the integer routing problem. The
solution method exploits the resulting asymmetry: the budget subproblem is
solved exactly, because the recourse value is convex in the budget at fixed
emissions, whereas the routing subproblem is addressed heuristically because
it is $\mathcal{NP}$-hard. The guarantee the method carries is accordingly
confined to the budget step, which returns a globally optimal allocation for
the emissions it is given.

Three implications for practice follow from the structure rather than from
any particular instance. First, the depot price $\lambda_d$ is an
interpretable internal carbon price: it measures what one further unit of
emission at that depot costs the firm, and it is confined to the band set by
the external purchase and sale prices, so it can be used as a transfer price
without reference to any accounting convention. Second, the divergence
between depot prices is bounded by the friction of the cheapest internal
transfer path between them, and a congested channel carries a strictly
positive multiplier. A firm that wishes its depots to face comparable carbon
incentives must therefore act on the transfer network itself, and the
multipliers indicate which channels to relieve. Third, the ordering of the
pooled, networked and independent regimes prices the internal controls that
restrict transfers: the difference between the networked and pooled values is
the operating cost of those controls, which converts a governance question
into a quantity the firm can measure. The same analysis identifies when
delegation does not matter -- if depots are homogeneous, or if credits can be
moved without friction, the allocation is cosmetic -- which is as useful to a
practitioner as the positive result.

The computational study is organised so that each claim rests on evidence of
the appropriate kind. Solution accuracy is assessed against exact optima
where instances admit them and against sample-average and progressive-hedging
benchmarks otherwise; the value of modelling the allocation stochastically is
measured out of sample rather than in sample, so that an in-sample advantage
which does not survive on held-out scenarios is reported as overfitting
rather than as value; and the interaction between the two recourse channels
is estimated on a balanced factorial design in which instance size is held
fixed. Mechanism-activation diagnostics are reported before any performance
comparison, so that degenerate parameter regimes remain visible instead of
being removed once results are known.

The framework is limited to a single planning period, a finite scenario set,
centralised decision making, and exogenous carbon prices. Multi-period
banking and borrowing, endogenous incentives for depot managers,
distributionally robust demand models, and decentralised depot behaviour are
natural extensions. None is a minor addition: each changes the coordination
problem itself, since banking couples periods, incentives make the depot
response endogenous, and decentralisation removes the single decision maker
on which the present formulation rests.

\section{PPBRC implementation details}
\label{app:ppbrc_details}

\subsection{Initial routes and initial budget}
Initial routes are built by cheapest-feasible insertion under capacity and route-duration constraints, opening a vehicle when no feasible insertion exists; complete PPBRC repeats this construction from several random customer orders and retains the best. The initial budget is 2-opt improvement. The reference-emission budget is
\[
 B_d^0=B^{\mathrm{corp}}\frac{\sum_\omega\pi_\omega E_d^{0,\omega}}{\sum_{h\in\mathcal D}\sum_\omega\pi_\omega E_h^{0,\omega}},
\]
then projected onto the bounded simplex defined by \eqref{eq:budget_total}--\eqref{eq:budget_bounds}.

\subsection{Neighbourhood evaluation}
A candidate is evaluated by recomputing the affected routes, so that the change in payload-distance emissions is accounted for exactly rather than approximated by a distance increment. Every candidate is checked against depot eligibility, vehicle capacity, route duration, and fleet availability. Within an outer iteration the price vector is fixed, so a candidate is scored by the price-linearised objective of Section~\ref{subsec:price_neighbourhoods} and no recourse problem is solved inside the neighbourhood loop; the exact recourse oracle is called once per price refit and once per outer iteration to evaluate the complete objective.

\subsection{Canonical recourse flows and the pooled regime}
The primary recourse solve minimises economic cost.  If its optimal flow is non-unique, a secondary solve fixes the primary optimum within tolerance and minimises total internal transfer.  This lexicographic rule removes gratuitous cycles and makes transfer-use statistics reproducible.  The fully pooled institutional benchmark is evaluated directly by \eqref{eq:pooled_recourse}; no depot-to-depot flow statistic is inferred from a zero-cost complete transfer graph.

\subsection{Stopping and reproducibility}
The implementation records random seeds, solver versions, hardware, time limits, the number of outer iterations $R$, the number of price refits $L$, the neighbourhood-search iteration count, and the number of oracle calls. Parameter values are calibrated on the separate tuning instances of Section~\ref{subsubsec:test_instances} and are never selected on instances whose results are reported.

\section{Instance and scenario generation}
\label{app:scenario_generation}

\paragraph{Literature basis}
The generator follows established conventions rather than ad hoc choices where a convention exists.  Depot placement and the core/boundary customer split follow the classical multi-depot geometry of \citet{cordeau1997vrp}; the clustered component of customer generation follows the controlled clustered-instance methodology of \citet{uchoa2017benchmark}, adopted here specifically to avoid instances that are ``too homogeneous'' in the sense that motivates their benchmark.  The correlated-shock demand model (a common depot-level factor plus an idiosyncratic customer term) is the standard risk-decomposition device used throughout the stochastic-routing literature surveyed by \citet{gendreau1996stochastic}.  The load-dependent emission function is the fuel-consumption model of \citet{bektas2011pollution} (already used as a comparison point in Table~\ref{tab:literature_taxonomy}), so the same emission mechanism grounds both the literature review and the instance generator.  Parameters internal to this paper's own experimental design -- the E2 carbon-network strata, the E4 factor levels, and the specific numeric spread/heterogeneity constants not fixed by the conventions above -- are calibration choices rather than literature-sourced values; they are recorded in full alongside each instance (see the validation protocol described below) rather than only in this prose, so they remain auditable.

\paragraph{Geometry and eligibility}
Depots are placed on a perturbed regular polygon in a $10\times10$ Euclidean region, following the classical multi-depot layouts of \citet{cordeau1997vrp}.  Core customers are drawn from depot-centred Gaussian clusters, the clustered-generation device of \citet{uchoa2017benchmark}.  Boundary customers are drawn around the midpoint of adjacent depots and are eligible for the corresponding depot pair; all other customers have only their home depot.  The requested boundary share is fixed before coordinates are generated.  Depot demand scales are heterogeneous and are generated independently of the scenario streams.

\paragraph{Demand streams}
Base demands are positive lognormal draws with depot-specific scales.  Optimisation, validation, final test, and distribution-shift scenarios use random-stream bases $2{,}000{,}000$, $3{,}000{,}000$, $4{,}000{,}000$, and $5{,}000{,}000$, respectively; geometry uses the disjoint base $1{,}000{,}000$.  The same instance index is added to each base, so every stream can be reproduced from the public seed manifest.  The validation and final test banks follow \eqref{eq:scenario_generator}, whose common-factor-plus-idiosyncratic-shock structure is the standard stochastic-demand decomposition surveyed by \citet{gendreau1996stochastic}.  The supplementary shift bank replaces Gaussian depot shocks by standardised Student-$t_5$ shocks and adds a low-probability asymmetric depot-demand regime.  It is used only to test distributional sensitivity.

For the stochastic-value suite, the training samples of size 20, 50, and 100 are nested prefixes of one bank.  This construction attributes changes in performance to sample size rather than to different random draws.  The validation bank may be inspected for selecting the sample size and stopping settings.  The sealed test bank is evaluated only after all algorithmic choices have been fixed.  Exact row overlap among training, validation, and test banks is required to be zero.

\paragraph{Carbon parameters and transfer network}
Reference emissions are obtained from deterministic home-depot angle-sweep routes under base demand, using the load-dependent fuel-consumption model of \citet{bektas2011pollution}.  Equation~\eqref{eq:budget_stringency} sets the corporate budget, and depot bounds are $0.45$ and $1.60$ times the reference allocation.  The transfer network is a bidirectional ring, augmented by two bidirectional chords when $|\mathcal D|=5$.  Arc costs contain fixed directional asymmetry.  Arc capacity is proportional to the mean reference emission of its two incident depots and the pre-specified capacity scale.  No parameter is recalibrated after final-instance performance is observed.

\paragraph{Validation protocol}
Every instance is checked before it is used, not after algorithm performance has been observed.  The checks cover dimensional consistency with the requested factor settings; positivity of coordinates, demands, and capacities; individual-vehicle feasibility of every scenario demand; fleet capacity slack under mean demand; feasibility of the depot budget bounds relative to the corporate budget; and separation of the random streams used for geometry and for the training, validation, test, and distribution-shift draws, so that no draw is reused across roles.  An instance failing any check is regenerated; it is never silently dropped after results are known.  Section~\ref{app:verification_checklist} describes the corresponding checks applied to every reported solution.  The generation code, the full instance set, and the random seeds needed to reproduce every draw are available in the repository accompanying this paper.

\section{Verification checklist}
\label{app:verification_checklist}

For every reported solution, the verification program recomputes route costs, payload flows, emissions, account balances, and recourse objectives from the saved routes and budgets. It checks the primal and dual recourse residuals, complementary slackness within tolerance, the KKT residual of \eqref{eq:expected_price_kkt}, and the absence of simultaneous external purchase and sale above tolerance.  For canonical-flow reporting it also checks that the secondary total-transfer objective is optimal on the primary recourse-optimal face.  Small instances are compared with the deterministic-equivalent MILP or exhaustive route enumeration where feasible. Results failing any check are excluded and reported as implementation errors rather than feasible observations.
